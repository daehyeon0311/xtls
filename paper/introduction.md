# 1. Introduction

> Draft. Korean translation in `introduction_ko.md`.

---

An integer-spin magnet with strong easy-plane anisotropy has two candidate
ground states. If the exchange dominates, the spins order. If the anisotropy
dominates, each site is driven into its S_z = 0 singlet and the system becomes a
quantum paramagnet with no magnetic order at any temperature. The two are
separated by a critical ratio of the single-ion anisotropy D to the exchange J,
and a material sitting close to that ratio offers access to the transition
between them.

The melilites A₂MX₂O₇ provide a well-characterized setting. The M²⁺ ions occupy
compressed MO₄ tetrahedra arranged on a square lattice, with A-site slabs
separating the layers, so the magnetism is quasi-two-dimensional. Ba₂FeSi₂O₇ is
the S = 2 member: the compression along c splits the ⁵E manifold and, through
second-order spin–orbit coupling, produces an easy-plane anisotropy of order
1 meV against an exchange an order of magnitude smaller [Jang, Do]. Inelastic
neutron scattering places it at D/J = 16.0 against a critical value of 19.0,
that is, at 84% of the way to the transition, with a correspondingly reduced
ordered moment [Do].

How to cover the remaining distance is then the practical question. A recent
X-ray linear dichroism study addressed it by determining D from full-multiplet
cluster calculations for both Ba₂FeSi₂O₇ and Sr₂FeSi₂O₇, exploiting the
different tetrahedral compressions of the two compounds, and extrapolating
along that structural trend [XLD]. Holding J fixed at its measured value, it
predicted that D would reach the critical anisotropy at a distortion angle of
about 10°, and proposed strain control as the route.

That prediction rests on J being insensitive to the distortion, which was
argued qualitatively rather than computed: two competing effects on the
superexchange were expected to cancel. Since the ground state is set by D/J and
not by D alone, the assumption carries the conclusion, and it has not been
tested.

Here we compute both quantities for the same deformation. The anisotropy comes
from a charge-transfer cluster model and the exchange from broken-symmetry
density-functional theory, with the two placed in a common convention. Applied
to the experimental structure they reproduce the measured parameters, giving
D/J = 15.9 against 16.0 and locating the material correctly on the ordered side
of the boundary.

Applied to strained structures they give a different picture from the published
one. Under uniaxial strain along c, J varies by 68% across four degrees of
distortion while D varies by 2%, so D/J *falls* as the tetrahedron is
compressed further. The transition is reached in the opposite direction, by
tensile strain of at most +4.7% along c.

We trace the discrepancy to a distinction that has not been drawn explicitly:
"distortion" can be varied either by A-site substitution or by uniaxial strain,
and the two move the Fe–O distance in opposite directions. The earlier D(Δθ)
was evaluated along the first and applied to the second. Since the A-site
cannot be substituted continuously past the Ba end-member, and since
Sr₂FeSi₂O₇ is *less* distorted than Ba₂FeSi₂O₇ rather than more, the chemical
route does not approach the boundary at all; strain is the only available one.

Finally we ask which other members of the family could host the transition, and
find that Kramers degeneracy excludes the half-integer-spin ions regardless of
their anisotropy, while Ni²⁺ retains an orbital degeneracy that leaves no
single-ion anisotropy defined. Fe²⁺ stands alone.
